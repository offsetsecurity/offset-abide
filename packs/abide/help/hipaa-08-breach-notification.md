# Breach notification (164.400 to 164.414)

The Breach Notification Rule says what to do when unsecured protected health information is compromised: decide whether it is a breach, then tell the people affected, HHS and sometimes the media, within fixed deadlines.

It covers protected health information in any form, including paper, not only ePHI.

Part of [the audit-ready handbook](hipaa-01-how-compliance-is-checked.md). Previous: [Policies and documentation](hipaa-07-documentation.md). Next: [Required documents and records](hipaa-09-required-documents.md).

The section numbers are from 45 CFR Part 164. The headings and the wording under each are ours, not the regulation's.

## Unsecured means not encrypted or destroyed

The Rule applies only to **unsecured** protected health information: information that has not been made unusable, unreadable or indecipherable to unauthorised people by a method HHS's guidance accepts. The accepted methods are encryption meeting the NIST standards the guidance names, with the key kept separately, and destruction. Password protection alone is not encryption.

## Three exceptions

Some unauthorised uses are not breaches at all:

1. A workforce member or agent acquires or uses the information unintentionally, in good faith and within the scope of their authority, and does not use or disclose it further.
2. A person authorised to access the information discloses it by mistake to another person authorised at the same organisation (or its business associate, or an organised health care arrangement it is part of), and it goes no further.
3. You have a good-faith belief that the person it was disclosed to could not reasonably have kept it.

Write down which exception applied and why. The burden of proof is yours.

## The deadlines at a glance

| Who you tell | When | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Each affected individual | Without unreasonable delay, and no later than 60 days after discovery | 164.404 |
| Prominent media in a state or jurisdiction | Same deadline, when more than 500 residents there are affected | 164.406 |
| HHS, 500 or more people | Same deadline, through the HHS breach portal | 164.408 |
| HHS, fewer than 500 people | Within 60 days of the end of the calendar year | 164.408 |
| The covered entity, if you are a business associate | Without unreasonable delay, and no later than 60 days after discovery | 164.410 |

Sixty days is the outer limit, not a target. Discovery is the first day the breach is known, or would have been known with reasonable diligence, to anyone in your workforce other than the person who caused it. State laws may set shorter deadlines.

Read [The first day of a suspected breach](breach-day-one.md) before you need it.

## 164.402 What counts as a breach

**Purpose.** The test for whether an incident is a breach: unsecured patient data has been acquired, accessed, used or disclosed in a way the Privacy Rule does not permit - and it is presumed to be a breach unless you can show a low probability that the data was compromised.

**What to do.** For every incident, run the four-factor assessment in writing: what data was involved and how identifiable; who saw it; whether it was actually acquired or viewed; and how far the risk has been reduced. Keep the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

**What OCR looks for.** The burden is on you. A decision of 'not a breach' with no written four-factor assessment behind it is treated as a breach you failed to report.

**Have ready.** Four-factor assessment per incident, dated and signed off; the decision and who made it.

## 164.404 Notice to the individuals affected

**Purpose.** Telling the people whose data was involved, without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery.

**What to do.** Write to each person by first-class post, or by email if they agreed to that. Say what happened, when, what data was involved, what they should do, what you are doing, and how to contact you. Where you cannot reach ten or more people, put a substitute notice on your website for 90 days or in the media.

**What OCR looks for.** The date of discovery against the date of the letters. Discovery is when any employee knew or should have known, not when management was told.

**Have ready.** Copy of the letter; the mailing list and date; substitute notice evidence where used.

## 164.406 Notice to the media

**Purpose.** For a breach affecting more than 500 residents of a state or jurisdiction, telling prominent media outlets there - within the same 60 days.

**What to do.** Prepare a press statement alongside the individual letters. Same content requirements. Do not wait for the story to break first.

**What OCR looks for.** Large breaches where individuals were told but the media notice was skipped.

**Have ready.** Press release; the outlets it went to; the date.

## 164.408 Notice to the Secretary of HHS

**Purpose.** Reporting breaches to HHS - immediately for 500 or more people, annually for smaller ones.

**What to do.** 500 or more: report through the HHS portal within 60 days of discovery. Fewer than 500: log each one and submit them within 60 days of the end of the calendar year. Keep the confirmation.

**What OCR looks for.** The small-breach log. Organisations remember the big report and forget the annual submission of the small ones.

**Have ready.** Portal submission confirmations; the running log of small breaches for the year.

## 164.410 Notice by a business associate

**Purpose.** A vendor who has a breach must tell the covered entity, without unreasonable delay and within 60 days of discovery.

**What to do.** If you are a vendor, know who to call at each customer and report in writing, with the individuals affected and everything you know. If you are the covered entity, put a shorter timescale in your agreements. Where the vendor acts as your agent, its discovery counts as yours, so its 60 days and yours run at the same time.

**What OCR looks for.** Contracts that simply restate 60 days, leaving the covered entity no time to act.

**Have ready.** Reporting route per customer; the notice sent, with dates; the contract clause.

## 164.412 Law enforcement delay

**Purpose.** Notification can be held back if law enforcement says it would impede an investigation.

**What to do.** If asked to delay, get it in writing with the period stated. If asked verbally, record it and delay no more than 30 days unless you then get it in writing.

**What OCR looks for.** A delay claimed with nothing in the file to support it.

**Have ready.** The written request, or a note of the verbal one with the name, agency and date.

## 164.414 Administrative requirements and burden of proof

**Purpose.** You must have the policies, training and complaint handling behind all this - and if you decide not to notify, it is on you to prove that decision was right.

**What to do.** Keep the breach procedure, train staff to report suspected breaches, and keep every assessment - including the ones that concluded no notification was needed.

**What OCR looks for.** The assessments behind decisions not to notify. They are the first thing asked for after a complaint.

**Have ready.** Breach policy; training records; every four-factor assessment kept for six years.
