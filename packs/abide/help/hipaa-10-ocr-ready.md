# Being ready for OCR

The last page of the handbook. There is no scheduled audit to prepare for:
OCR contacts you when a complaint, a breach report or a review brings you to its
attention, and asks for documents with a deadline. Being ready means you could
answer that letter from what you already have.

Part of [the audit-ready handbook](hipaa-01-how-compliance-is-checked.md).
Previous: [Required documents and records](hipaa-09-required-documents.md).

## What OCR typically asks for

The request is shaped by what brought OCR to you, but these come up again and
again:

- your risk analysis, the current one and earlier versions,
- your risk management plan, and evidence of progress on it,
- the policies and procedures that were in effect at the time of the incident,
- evidence of workforce training, with dates,
- business associate agreements with the vendors involved,
- the incident record: what happened, when it was discovered, what you did,
- the breach risk assessment, and copies of any notifications,
- audit logs or activity reviews relevant to the incident,
- sanctions applied to anyone involved,
- your decisions on addressable specifications that relate to the incident,
  such as encryption.

Note "in effect at the time". Keep old versions of policies, not only the
current one.

## The readiness checklist

### Foundations

- A security official is named in writing, and still works here.
- A risk analysis covers every system, device and vendor that touches ePHI,
  and has been reviewed in the last year or since the last major change.
- A risk management plan turns each significant risk into work with an owner
  and a date.
- Every addressable specification has a written decision and reason.

### Policies

- Written policies and procedures cover every standard and specification.
- They are approved, dated, and reviewed on a schedule.
- Staff can find them, and have acknowledged them.
- Old versions are kept for six years.

### People

- Everyone in the workforce, including managers and clinicians, has had
  security training, with records.
- Security reminders go out between training rounds.
- Access is approved before it is granted, reviewed periodically, and removed
  the day someone leaves.
- The sanction policy is known and has been applied when needed.

### Systems

- Every user has their own account; no shared logins remain.
- Multi-factor authentication protects remote access, email and administrator
  accounts.
- Laptops and other portable devices are encrypted, or the decision not to is
  written down with an alternative.
- Audit logging is on, protected, kept long enough, and reviewed.
- Backups include everything with ePHI, with an offline or immutable copy, and a
  restore has been tested.
- Emergency access exists, is alerted on use, and each use is reviewed.

### Vendors

- Every business associate is listed, with a signed agreement dated before
  their access began.
- Each agreement requires safeguards, subcontractor terms and incident reporting
  with a stated number of days.

### Incidents and breaches

- Staff know how to report an incident.
- Every incident is logged, including attempts and near misses.
- Every incident involving protected health information has a written
  four-factor assessment.
- Notifications went out within the deadlines, and the records are kept.
- The annual submission of small breaches to HHS is in the calendar.

### Checking it

- A periodic evaluation of your safeguards has been done, and its gaps are being
  closed.
- The contingency plan has been tested, and changed where the test showed a
  problem.

**In Offset Abide.** **Get ready** tracks most of this list. The **Gap report**
and **Gap analysis** show what is still missing.

## What OCR settlements commonly cite

These themes recur in the resolution agreements OCR publishes:

- **No accurate and thorough risk analysis**, or one limited to part of the
  environment.
- **No risk management**: risks identified and then left alone.
- **No business associate agreement** with a vendor that handled ePHI.
- **Weak access controls**: shared accounts, excessive access, leavers not
  removed.
- **No review of system activity**, so improper access went unnoticed.
- **No encryption** on a device that was then lost or stolen, with no written
  reason for the decision.
- **Late breach notification.**

Check each of these yourself before anyone else does.

## If OCR writes to you

1. **Note the deadline** in the letter, and diary it.
2. **Tell your leadership, and get legal advice.** An OCR inquiry is a legal
   matter.
3. **Preserve everything.** Suspend any routine deletion of logs, emails and
   records that might be relevant.
4. **Gather what existed at the time.** Send the documents as they were, with
   their dates.
5. **Never create or back-date a document** to fill a gap. If something does not
   exist, say so, and describe what you are doing about it. A fabricated record
   turns a compliance problem into a much worse one.
6. **Answer what is asked**, completely and on time.
7. **Fix what the inquiry exposes**, and record the corrective action. Being able
   to show that problems were found and fixed helps.

## A yearly cycle

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Every month | Review access logs and failed sign-ins; check **Calendar** for reviews falling due; work through open **Tasks** and **Findings** |
| Every quarter | Access reviews; a restore test; send security reminders; review open risks |
| Every year | Update the risk analysis; train the whole workforce; review every policy; evaluate your safeguards; test the contingency plan; review business associate agreements |
| By 1 March | Submit the previous calendar year's breaches affecting fewer than 500 people to HHS (60 days after the end of the year; 29 February in a leap year) |
| After any significant change | Update the risk analysis and evaluate the safeguards it affects |
