# The first day of a suspected breach

Read this now, not on the day. The clock is shorter than people think and it
starts earlier than they hope.

---

## The clock

**60 calendar days from discovery.** Not 60 days from when you finished
investigating, or from when the lawyers agreed, or from when management was
told.

**Discovery is when any member of your workforce knew, or reasonably should
have known.** A front-desk clerk who noticed an odd email on the 3rd starts the
clock on the 3rd, whether or not they told anybody.

That is why the reporting route matters more than the procedure: every day
between someone noticing and someone acting is a day off your 60.

---

## Hour one: stop it getting worse

1. **Contain.** Disconnect the machine, disable the account, stop the process.
   Do not turn the machine off if it may hold evidence in memory and you have
   anyone who can capture it.
2. **Do not destroy the evidence.** Rebuilding the laptop is the fastest way to
   lose the answer to "what did they take", which is exactly what the
   four-factor assessment needs.
3. **Write down the time.** When it was noticed, by whom, and what they saw.
   This single line decides your deadline.
4. **Tell your Security Official.** One named person, as 164.308(a)(2) requires.

Record it in **Findings** straight away, even before you know what it is.
An incident that turns out to be nothing still belongs in the log — an empty
incident log reads as nobody reporting, not as nothing happening.

---

## Day one: work out what it is

Every incident is a security incident. Only some are breaches.

A breach is unsecured patient data acquired, accessed, used or disclosed in a
way the Privacy Rule does not permit. **It is presumed to be a breach** unless
you can show a low probability that the data was compromised.

That presumption is the whole point. You do not have to prove harm; you have to
prove the opposite, in writing.

### The four factors

Write an answer to each. Keep the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

1. **What data, and how identifiable?** Names with diagnoses, or a spreadsheet
   of appointment times? Social Security numbers, or initials?
2. **Who was it disclosed to?** Another covered entity bound by HIPAA is a
   different risk from an unknown person on the internet.
3. **Was it actually acquired or viewed?** A laptop recovered with forensics
   showing it was never powered on is different from one that vanished.
4. **How far has the risk been reduced?** Returned unopened, confirmed deleted,
   a signed attestation from the recipient.

Then the exceptions worth knowing:

- **Encryption.** Data encrypted to the HHS standard is not "unsecured", so it
  is generally not a reportable breach at all. This is why encryption, an
  addressable item, decides more outcomes than almost anything else.
- Unintentional access by a workforce member acting in good faith, within
  their role, and going no further.
- Inadvertent disclosure between two authorised people at the same
  organisation, going no further.
- A disclosure where you have a good-faith belief the recipient could not have
  retained the information.

---

## What you must do, and when

| Who | When |
|---|---|
| The individuals affected | Without unreasonable delay, no later than 60 days from discovery |
| HHS, if 500 or more people | Within 60 days of discovery, through the HHS portal |
| HHS, if fewer than 500 | In the annual submission, within 60 days of the year end |
| Prominent media, if more than 500 in one state | Within the same 60 days |
| Your customer, if you are the business associate | Without unreasonable delay, within 60 days — and your contract may say less |

**The letter has to say:** what happened and when, what data was involved, what
the person should do to protect themselves, what you are doing about it, and
how to contact you. First-class post, or email if they have agreed to that.

If you cannot reach ten or more people, you also need a substitute notice: on
your website for 90 days, or in print or broadcast media.

---

## The part people get wrong

**The annual small-breach submission.** Under 500 people means you log it and
report it after the year ends — and that is the one that gets forgotten, every
year, until an investigator asks for the log.

Put it in the **Calendar** now, dated 60 days after your year end.

**Deciding it is not a breach, with nothing written down.** The burden of proof
is yours. A decision with no four-factor assessment behind it is treated, later,
as a breach you failed to report.

Keep every assessment for six years. Including — especially — the ones that
concluded no notification was needed.

---

## Afterwards

- Fix the cause, not just the symptom. Raise it in **Tasks** with an owner.
- Update the risk analysis if it showed you something new.
- Write down what you learned. The same incident happening twice is a much
  worse conversation than the first one.
