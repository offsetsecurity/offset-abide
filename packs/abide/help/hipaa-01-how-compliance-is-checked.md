# How HIPAA compliance is checked

This handbook takes you through the HIPAA Security Rule and the Breach
Notification Rule in the order the regulation sets them out: the general rules,
then the administrative, physical, technical and organisational safeguards,
then documentation, then breach notification. After that come the documents you
must keep and how to be ready when the Office for Civil Rights (OCR) asks for
them.

The wording in this handbook is ours. It explains the regulation; it is not the
regulation, and it is not legal advice. The rules are in 45 CFR Part 164,
Subparts C and D, free on the eCFR website. Where this handbook and the
regulation differ, the regulation wins.

## There is no HIPAA certificate

No government body certifies HIPAA compliance, and no private company can do it
on the government's behalf. A vendor offering "HIPAA certification" is selling
its own opinion. What you can have is a compliance file: written policies, a
current risk analysis, and records showing you do what they say. That file is
what OCR asks for.

## Who the rules apply to

| Who | What they are | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Covered entities | Health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers who send health information electronically in standard transactions | Hospitals, clinics, practices, pharmacies, insurers, clearinghouses |
| Business associates | Organisations that create, receive, keep or send protected health information on behalf of a covered entity | Billing companies, IT support, cloud hosting, transcription, shredding |
| Subcontractors | A business associate's own suppliers who handle the same information | The cloud platform behind a billing company |

Business associates are directly liable for meeting the Security Rule. They are
not only bound by contract.

## What the Security Rule protects

**Electronic protected health information (ePHI)**: individually identifiable
health information that you create, receive, keep or send in electronic form.
Paper records and spoken conversations are covered by the Privacy Rule, not the
Security Rule.

The Rule asks you to protect three things about ePHI:

- **Confidentiality**: only the right people see it.
- **Integrity**: it is not changed or destroyed improperly.
- **Availability**: it is there when care needs it.

## Required and addressable

Each standard in the Security Rule has implementation specifications, and each
specification is labelled one of two ways:

- **Required**: do it.
- **Addressable**: assess whether it is reasonable and appropriate for you. If
  it is, do it. If it is not, write down why, and do something equivalent
  instead if that is reasonable and appropriate.

Addressable never means optional. It means you decide, and you write the
decision down with your reasons. An addressable item with no written decision
is treated as not done. [The general rules](hipaa-02-general-rules.md) explain
this in full.

## Who enforces it, and how they find you

**OCR**, part of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS),
enforces the Privacy, Security and Breach Notification Rules. State attorneys
general can also bring HIPAA actions.

OCR usually becomes involved through:

- **a complaint**, from a patient, an employee or anyone else,
- **a breach report**, including the ones you are required to file yourself,
- **a compliance review**, which OCR can open on its own initiative,
- **an audit**, under OCR's audit programme.

Every breach report affecting 500 or more people is reviewed by OCR.

## What happens if you fall short

Most cases OCR looks into are closed with technical assistance, or with
corrective action by the organisation, without a fine. The serious ones end in
a **resolution agreement**, which is a payment plus a corrective action plan
that OCR monitors for a period of years, or in **civil money penalties**.

Penalties are set in four tiers by how culpable you were, from not knowing and
not being able to know, up to wilful neglect left uncorrected. The amounts are
adjusted for inflation every year; check hhs.gov for the current figures.
Criminal penalties exist for knowingly obtaining or disclosing health
information in breach of the law, and are pursued by the Department of Justice.

## Recognised security practices

Since a 2021 amendment to the HITECH Act, OCR must take into account whether you
had **recognised security practices** in place for the previous 12 months when
it decides fines, audit outcomes and other remedies. Recognised practices
include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and the practices published under
section 405(d) of the Cybersecurity Act of 2015. Keep evidence that they were in
place, over time. It does not excuse noncompliance, but it can reduce the
consequences.

## The proposed update to the Security Rule

In January 2025, HHS proposed a major update to the Security Rule. Among other
changes, it would end the difference between required and addressable, and
require encryption, multi-factor authentication, an asset inventory and a
network map, and regular testing.

It is a proposal, not law. As of mid 2026 it had not been made final, and HHS's
regulatory agenda showed a final rule no earlier than 2027. The final version
may differ from the proposal. This handbook describes the rules in force. Check
hhs.gov for the current status before relying on either.

## What this product does not cover

**The Privacy Rule.** The notice of privacy practices, the minimum necessary
standard, patient access and amendment requests, and accounting of
disclosures. These are HIPAA requirements too, but they are outside what Offset
Abide manages.

**State law.** Many states have their own breach notification and health
privacy laws, some stricter than HIPAA. Where a state law is stricter, you must
meet it as well.
